Worksheet: Working with Confidence Intervals in the UK LFS Data

This worksheet will guide you through working with confidence intervals (CIs) using the UK Quarterly Labour Force Survey (LFS) October-December 2023 data (od24). You will learn how to compare weighted and unweighted estimates, compare weighted CIs across regions, and adjust CIs using design effects.

Exercise 1: Comparing Weighted and Unweighted Estimates of Mean Gross Weekly Earnings

Introduction

In survey data, weighting is often used to ensure that estimates are representative of the population. Here, we compare the weighted and unweighted estimates of mean gross weekly earnings (GRSSWK) and their confidence intervals. The weighting variable is PIWT24.

R Syntax

# Load necessary libraries
library(survey)
library(dplyr)
library(haven)

od24 <- read_dta(
        "~/Data/lfs/UKDA-9349-stata/stata/stata13/lfsp_od24_eul_pwt24.dta"
                    )

# Calculate unweighted mean and CI
unweighted_mean <- mean(od24$GRSSWK, na.rm = TRUE)
unweighted_ci <- t.test(od24$GRSSWK)$conf.int

# Create survey design object
lfs_design <- svydesign(ids = ~1, weights = ~PIWT24, data = od24)

# Calculate weighted mean and CI
weighted_mean <- svymean(~GRSSWK, design = lfs_design, na.rm = TRUE)
weighted_ci <- confint(weighted_mean)

Output

# Print results
cat("Unweighted Mean:", unweighted_mean, "\n")
Unweighted Mean: 75.80171 
cat("Unweighted CI:", unweighted_ci, "\n")
Unweighted CI: 73.33218 78.27124 
cat("Weighted Mean:", weighted_mean, "\n")
Weighted Mean: 693.7501 
cat("Weighted CI:", weighted_ci, "\n")
Weighted CI: 681.8465 705.6538

Interpretation

The unweighted mean and CI do not account for the survey design, while the weighted mean and CI use the weighting variable PIWT24 to produce representative estimates. The weighted CI is typically wider due to the incorporation of design effects.

Questions

  1. Why is the weighted CI wider than the unweighted CI?
  2. What does the weighting variable PIWT24 represent?
  3. How might ignoring weights affect the interpretation of the results?

Answers

  1. The weighted CI is wider because it accounts for the survey design, including stratification and clustering, which increases the variability of the estimate.
  2. PIWT24 is the person-level weight for the survey, ensuring the sample represents the population.
  3. Ignoring weights can lead to biased estimates that do not reflect the true population parameters.

Exercise 2: Comparing Weighted CIs of Proportions Across Regions

Introduction

In this exercise, we compare the weighted confidence intervals of the proportion of female employees who self-reported working part-time (FTPT) in London and the North West. The regional variable is GOVTOF2.

R Syntax

# Filter data for females and part-time workers
female_parttime <- od24 %>% filter(SEX == 2 & FTPT == 2)

# Create survey design object
lfs_design <- svydesign(ids = ~1, weights = ~PIWT24, data = female_parttime)

# Calculate weighted proportions and CIs for London and North West
london_prop <- svymean(~GOVTOF2 == 7, design = lfs_design)
northwest_prop <- svymean(~GOVTOF2 == 2, design = lfs_design)

london_ci <- confint(london_prop)
northwest_ci <- confint(northwest_prop)

Output

# Print results
cat("London Proportion:", london_prop, "\n")
London Proportion: 0.9089568 0.09104316 
cat("London CI:", london_ci, "\n")
London CI: 0.8916423 0.07372865 0.9262713 0.1083577 
cat("North West Proportion:", northwest_prop, "\n")
North West Proportion: 0.8962006 0.1037994 
cat("North West CI:", northwest_ci, "\n")
North West CI: 0.8774707 0.08506962 0.9149304 0.1225293

Interpretation

The weighted proportions and CIs show the estimated proportion of female part-time workers in London and the North West. The CIs provide a range within which the true proportion is likely to lie.

Questions

  1. What does the overlap (or lack thereof) between the CIs for London and the North West indicate?
  2. How does the sample size in each region affect the width of the CI?
  3. Why is it important to use weights when comparing regions?

Answers

  1. Overlapping CIs suggest no statistically significant difference between the proportions, while non-overlapping CIs indicate a significant difference.
  2. Larger sample sizes result in narrower CIs, as they provide more precise estimates.
  3. Weights ensure that the estimates are representative of the population in each region, accounting for differences in sampling probabilities.

Exercise 3: Adjusting CIs Using Design Effects

Introduction

Design effects account for the impact of survey design (e.g., clustering and stratification) on the precision of estimates. Here, we adjust the CI of the proportion of people in employment (ILODEFR) by age bands (AGE) using design effects published in Volume 1 of the LFS documentation.

R Syntax

# Load design effects for age bands
design_effects <- c(1.2, 1.5, 1.8) # Example values for age bands 16-24, 25-34, 35+

# Create survey design object
lfs_design <- svydesign(ids = ~1, weights = ~PIWT24, data = od24)

# Calculate weighted proportions and CIs by age band
age_props <- svyby(~ILODEFR == 1, ~AGE, design = lfs_design, svymean)

# Adjust CIs using design effects
adjusted_cis <- confint(age_props) * sqrt(design_effects)

Output

Interpretation

The adjusted CIs account for the design effects, which increase the width of the CIs to reflect the reduced precision due to the survey design. This provides a more accurate measure of uncertainty.

Questions

  1. What is a design effect, and why is it important?
  2. How do design effects impact the width of the CI?
  3. Why might design effects vary across age bands?

Answers

  1. A design effect measures how much the survey design increases the variance of an estimate compared to a simple random sample. It is important because it ensures accurate uncertainty estimates.
  2. Design effects increase the width of the CI, reflecting the reduced precision due to clustering and stratification.
  3. Design effects vary across age bands due to differences in sample sizes, clustering, and stratification within each age group.
